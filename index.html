Funding a Collection: Borrowing Against Several Classics at Once

Most writing about classic car finance assumes one buyer and one car. Collectors do not live like that. They own several cars, usually outright, and the decision in front of them is how to add another, or how to raise money for something else entirely, without breaking up what they have built.

That is a different problem and it has a different answer. A lender can treat a group of cars as a single pool of security.

From one car to a pool

On a single car agreement the lender values that car, advances a share of the value and takes security over it. A collection facility does the same across several cars at once. Each car is valued individually, the values are added together, and the lender advances against the total.

The money can then be used for whatever the owner needs. Buying another car is the obvious purpose. Others include funding a restoration, bridging the gap between buying one car and selling another, or releasing capital for use elsewhere. This is equity release applied to more than one vehicle.

A worked example

Take a collector with three cars owned outright, valued by an independent valuer at £180,000, £125,000 and £95,000. Together that is £400,000.

Deposits on classics, as published on our site, run from 25 to 40 per cent of an agreed valuation. Put the other way, a lender expects the owner to keep that much equity in the cars. Suppose the lender is prepared to advance £250,000 against the three, which leaves the owner with 37.5 per cent of the value unencumbered.

Repaid over 48 months at 9.9 per cent, £250,000 comes to about £6,329 a month, with roughly £53,775 of interest across the term. Over 60 months the payment would be about £5,299.

Representative example only. Rates vary by individual circumstances. This is not a formal offer of finance.

The owner keeps all three cars, keeps using them, and has £250,000 to spend. If that money buys a fourth car outright, the fourth car is unencumbered and can itself be offered as security later.

Why pool the security at all

Three reasons.

Spread. A lender looking at one unusual car has all its risk in a single valuation. A lender looking at three or four has that risk spread, and one car with thin comparables can be carried by others with strong ones. Cars that would be hard to fund alone can sometimes be included in a pool.

Simplicity. One agreement, one payment, one renewal date, in place of several.

Flexibility. A well drafted facility allows cars to be released from the security when sold and new cars to be added. The collection can change shape without the borrowing being rewritten each time.

How cars are added and released

This is the clause to read most carefully. In a typical arrangement, when you sell a car that forms part of the security, the lender releases its interest in exchange for a repayment. The repayment is usually the amount attributed to that car, or enough to keep the remaining borrowing within the agreed share of the remaining valuation.

Using the example above, selling the £95,000 car would reduce the pool to £305,000. To hold the borrowing at the same 62.5 per cent of value, it would need to fall to about £190,600, a repayment of roughly £59,400 from the sale proceeds. The rest of the sale price is the owner’s.

Substitution works in reverse. A newly acquired car is valued, added to the schedule, and either increases the available borrowing or replaces a car being released.

Not every lender offers this flexibility, and those that do document it differently. Ask for the release mechanism in writing before you sign.

The drawbacks of pooling

Cross security cuts both ways. Under a pooled facility, default puts every car in the pool at risk, not only the newest purchase. An owner who would rather ring fence a particular car, one with family history for example, should keep it out of the security.

Valuation costs are higher at the start, since every car needs a current report. And a pooled facility is a bespoke piece of lending. It takes longer to arrange than a single car agreement and, in our experience, fewer funders write it.

For those reasons a collection facility tends to make sense from three or four cars upwards. Below that, separate agreements on individual cars are often simpler and no dearer.

Personal ownership or a company

Collections are held in many ways: personally, through a limited company, in a partnership or in trust. A lender can work with any of them, but it needs to know which, because the borrower must be the party that owns the cars.

The structure also affects how the lending is documented. Business agreements entered into wholly or predominantly for business purposes are not regulated consumer credit. Where an individual is borrowing for personal reasons and the agreement is regulated, it is arranged through an FCA authorised broker partner. Tell us at the outset how the cars are owned and what the money is for.

What a lender wants for each car

The same as for a single classic, repeated for each vehicle: the V5C, photographs of chassis and engine numbers, an ownership history, the invoice file, evidence of originality and an independent valuation. Add to that a schedule of the whole collection with storage locations, and insurance on an agreed value basis for each car with the lender’s interest noted.

Collectors with tidy files find this stage straightforward. Published guides such as the Hagerty price guide give a valuer a starting range for each model, and the file is what positions each car within it.

Rates and terms

Across our lender panel the indicative band is 8.9 to 9.9 per cent and terms on classics typically run from 36 to 60 months. There is no minimum advance, although the work involved in a pooled facility means it suits larger sums.
